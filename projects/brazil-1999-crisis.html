Brazil 1999: The Samba Effect

A case study of Brazil's January 1999 currency crisis — tracing the collapse of the Real Plan's crawling-peg regime, the ~50% depreciation of the real against the dollar, and the policy response that prevented a lost decade. The analysis applies three-generation crisis theory to explain why Brazil escaped the fate of Argentina, which defended a similar peg until catastrophic collapse in 2001–02. Presented as part of the Financial Crises and Policy Dilemmas in Emerging Markets and Latin America course at Johns Hopkins SAIS (Fall 2025).

Key Findings

  • Brazil entered 1999 with five structural vulnerabilities: chronic fiscal deficits, an overvalued crawling-peg exchange rate, heavy external debt, a fragile banking sector, and contagion pressure from the Asian (1997) and Russian (1998) crises.
  • The crisis trigger was political, not purely macroeconomic — the Minas Gerais governor's January 6 moratorium on federal debt payments set off a self-fulfilling speculative attack that drained reserves and forced a float within days.
  • The real depreciated ~50% against the dollar in weeks, yet pass-through inflation reached only 8.9% by year-end — far below the 20%+ feared — because the Cardoso government rapidly deployed the "Macroeconomic Tripod": a floating exchange rate, a primary fiscal surplus target, and formal inflation targeting.
  • Brazil achieved a V-shaped recovery, returning to positive growth by 2000; Argentina, which defended its convertibility peg until 2001, suffered a deeper and more prolonged collapse — illustrating that the timing and credibility of policy adjustment is as important as the adjustment itself.
  • Five cross-cutting lessons: pegs require fiscal discipline to be durable; central bank independence is essential for post-devaluation credibility; IMF conditionality can anchor market expectations; early adjustment beats delayed collapse; and institutional capacity determines whether a crisis becomes a turning point or a lost decade.
