Lubumbashi: How Mining Rents Shape Urban Form

A case study examining how copper mining rents determine the spatial structure, labor market composition, and built environment of Lubumbashi — the DRC's second-largest city and the heart of the Central African Copperbelt. Drawing on the consumption-city framework and urban-inequality theory, the analysis tests whether Lubumbashi exhibits the hallmarks of a resource-dependent city: a workforce concentrated in non-tradable services, horizontal low-rise expansion, and a steep, segmented wage ladder. Presented as part of the Urban Economics for the Developing World course at Johns Hopkins SAIS (Fall 2025). Co-authored with Matthew Mangan and Oge Onubogu.

Key Findings

  • ~75% of Lubumbashi's workforce is employed in non-tradable sectors — the textbook signature of a "Consumption City" in which mining income drives service demand rather than manufacturing growth.
  • The city has expanded horizontally for four decades: median building height in the densest neighborhoods is just 3–5 meters, reflecting a low-verticality urban morphology shaped by colonial street geometry and mine geography.
  • Food import dependence is near-total — even basic foodstuffs are sourced from abroad — reinforcing the consumption-city profile and exposing residents to global commodity price shocks.
  • Lubumbashi's labor market is sharply stratified, with a continuous wage ladder from artisanal day-laborers to expatriate managers earning roughly 100× more, driving deep spatial inequality between mining-adjacent and peripheral neighborhoods.
  • 99% of smallholder fishers and farmers near smelter operations reported sharp yield declines from river and wetland contamination, concentrating an environmental burden on the city's lowest-income residents.
  • All three hypotheses were confirmed: the consumption-city signature, horizontal morphology, and a segmented wage ladder each align with theoretical predictions, suggesting that governance reforms — particularly channeling mining rents into city-wide public goods — are the most direct lever for breaking the resource trap.
